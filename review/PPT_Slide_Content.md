## 1. Title & Team Details

**Speaker notes:**

Good morning. Our project is the Policy Verification System. It answers questions about university policy, but instead of just giving an answer, it breaks the answer into individual claims, checks each one against the policy text it cites, and refuses to answer when it can't find real evidence. I'm ___, and with me are ___, ___ and ___.

**Likely questions:**

- **Why "claim-level"?** One sentence can be half right and half wrong, so each fact gets its own verdict instead of grading the whole answer at once (Slides 4 and 6).

## 2. Abstract & Introduction

**Speaker notes:**

Chatbots are very good at sounding right. For policy questions that's dangerous: if a bot tells a student the attendance requirement is 80% when it's actually 75%, and attaches a citation, the student has no easy way to know it's wrong. Our system doesn't just answer. It splits the answer into separate claims, and each claim is checked against the exact policy text it cites by a separate model that only asks one question: does this text prove this claim? Numbers are double-checked separately. Claims that fail are shown as rejected with the reason, and if nothing survives, the system says it can't answer. The box on the right is our verifier on three test claims: the correct 75% rule is supported, the wrong 80% is refuted, and an invented fine is marked unverified.

**Likely questions:**

- **Were these claims generated by the model?** No. We wrote them to test the verifier, and the slide says so. In a live run on this question, the model's three generated claims were all supported.
- **Why run locally instead of using GPT-4?** No per-question cost, policy data stays private, and runs are reproducible: temperature 0 and fixed model versions.
- **Do you have accuracy numbers?** Not yet. The evaluation harness is the next step; Slide 10 explains the plan.

## 3. Problem Statement & Objectives

**Speaker notes:**

The problem isn't that chatbots are always wrong. It's that the user can't tell which parts are right. We see three failure types: a wrong fact or figure, a citation that doesn't actually back the claim, and confident answers to questions the policy never covers. Our five objectives map onto the pipeline: retrieve with precise citations, generate one-fact claims, verify each claim, decide what to keep or reject, and evaluate honestly with a held-out split. Scope is deliberately narrow for this phase: six SRM documents, English text, local open models, no fine-tuning.

**Likely questions:**

- **Why only one university?** A deliberate scope decision: curating six documents by hand let us check every extraction. It does mean we can't yet test confusion between universities.
- **Why not fine-tune a model?** Fine-tuning needs labelled data we don't have, and our point is that verification should be independent of the generator.
- **Is the web interface part of this review?** No. The core pipeline is built; a Streamlit interface is planned for a later phase.

## 4. Literature Survey

**Speaker notes:**

We reviewed six papers, each covering one part of our design. RAG is the base architecture. FActScore shows why checking individual facts beats grading whole sentences. ALCE shows citations can be checked automatically with an NLI model, and that even GPT-4 often fails. MiniCheck shows a small, local checker can match GPT-4, and warns that standard NLI training data is too easy. Self-RAG is the main alternative: the model checks itself. Magesh et al. show that even commercial legal RAG tools still hallucinate. Every number here was checked against the original paper.

**Likely questions:**

- **Why these six and not more?** Each covers one role: foundation, method, evaluation metric, verifier choice, competing approach, and domain evidence. Detailed notes on these and supporting papers are in our literature review document.
- **Isn't MiniCheck's finding against claim decomposition a problem for you?** It's a real tension with FActScore. Our model already outputs one-fact claims, so we pay no extra decomposition cost, and claim-level verdicts let a user see which fact failed.
- **The legal study isn't about university policy. Why include it?** It's the closest published evidence for answering from authoritative rule documents with citations, and its 'misgrounded' category matches our citation-misuse case.

## 5. Research Gap & Proposed Contribution

**Speaker notes:**

Reading the six papers side by side, none combines four things: checking each claim, using a checker independent of the generator, enforcing the check at answer time, and working on institutional policy documents. FActScore and ALCE verify, but only as offline evaluation. Self-RAG checks at answer time, but the model grades itself. The legal audit is the closest domain, but it's a manual audit. Our proposal fills that combination. Status is shown honestly: two parts are built, citation-error typing is only partly connected in the pipeline, and the evaluation hasn't been run, so we make no performance claims.

**Likely questions:**

- **Is this gap really new?** We claim it only relative to the six papers we reviewed. Runtime-correction methods such as RARR and Chain-of-Verification still need reviewing before we claim novelty.
- **What does "partly built" mean for citation typing?** The checker can tell an invented section from a real one that doesn't support the claim, but the main pipeline currently strips unknown citations before that check runs. Fixing that is on our next-steps list.
- **Why does the numeric guard matter if NLI caught the 80% claim?** NLI caught it in our test, but NLI models are known to miss partial or subtle support (ALCE, MiniCheck). A deterministic number check is a cheap independent second opinion.

## 6. Proposed Methodology

**Speaker notes:**

The method has four stages: retrieve, generate, verify, decide. The table shows why we chose each component. Everything runs on one laptop: the embedding and NLI models on the CPU, so the 8 GB GPU is free for Qwen. The key design choice is that the model that writes the answer never grades it; a separate NLI model and a deterministic number check do. One honest note: when a claim fails, we remove it and explain why. We don't automatically rewrite it into a corrected claim.

**Likely questions:**

- **Why a 4B model and not something bigger?** It fits an 8 GB laptop GPU alongside the other models (measured peak 3.9 GB). The config allows a switch to Qwen3 8B, which we haven't evaluated yet.
- **How was the NLI model chosen?** A small comparison of three NLI models on 20 hand-written claim–passage pairs. It's a spot check; MiniCheck will be compared properly in the evaluation.
- **Why temperature 0?** So the same question gives the same answer; a system whose output changes between runs can't be evaluated or debugged.

## 7. High-Level Architecture

**Speaker notes:**

This is the full flow for one question, in four lanes. Every diamond is a real if-statement in our code. Retrieve: if the index was built with a different embedding model we stop; otherwise we fetch 24 candidates and keep 6, at most 2 per section. Generate: no passages means we skip the model; the model must return valid JSON, with one retry; citations to passages it never saw are flagged. Verify, for each claim: no citation means neutral; entailment of 0.5 or more plus matching numbers means supported; a number mismatch vetoes support; contradiction of 0.5 or more means refuted. Decide: we abstain if retrieval is weak, if there are no claims, or if no claim is kept.

**Likely questions:**

- **Why can the number check only block, never approve?** Matching digits isn't proof the passage means the same thing. Only the NLI model can approve; the number check can only veto, for example 80% vs 75%.
- **Why does the retrieval check happen after generation?** That's the current code order. Moving it earlier would save about 20 seconds on questions that end in abstention; it's on our improvement list.
- **Are the thresholds tuned?** No, they're defaults, as the diagram's legend says. They'll be tuned on the tuning split.

## 8. Low-Level Design: Preparation & Retrieval

**Speaker notes:**

Offline, we download six SRM policy documents, extract clean text, and split it into sections at real headings, so each section gets a precise citation like 7.3-minimum-attendance. Sections are cut into 258 chunks, embedded with bge-base, and stored in Chroma with their citation metadata. When a student asks a question, we embed it, fetch the closest chunks, cap each section at two, and pass the top six to the next stage.

**Likely questions:**

- **Why split at headings instead of fixed-size chunks?** So a citation points at one real section of the policy. A chunk spanning two rules could falsely support a claim that mixes them.
- **Why cap at two chunks per section?** In testing, one very long examination section filled all the top results and pushed the actual attendance rule to rank 4.
- **What if the embedding model changes?** The index records which model built it, and search refuses to run on a mismatch instead of silently returning wrong passages.

## 9. Low-Level Design: Generation & Verification

**Speaker notes:**

Qwen3 must answer as JSON: a list of single-fact claims, each citing a passage. We validate that structure, with one retry, and flag citations to passages it never saw. Each claim is then checked on its own: the NLI model tests whether the cited text entails or contradicts it, and the number check vetoes support if a figure doesn't match. The verdict is supported, refuted or neutral. Finally we keep only supported claims, show the rejected ones with reasons, and abstain if nothing survives.

**Likely questions:**

- **What if the model's JSON is broken?** We retry once with a stricter instruction, then stop with an error. We never guess at a malformed answer.
- **What does 'correction' mean here?** A rejected claim is removed and shown with the reason. We don't rewrite it.
- **How are long passages handled by the NLI model?** They're split into 900-character overlapping windows so nothing is silently truncated; support anywhere in the passage counts.

## 10. Evaluation Plan & Metrics

**Speaker notes:**

On the left is what's done and what isn't. The pipeline is built and tested, and the 64-question adversarial set exists, but we have not run the evaluation yet, so there are no accuracy numbers in this presentation. The plan: tune thresholds on 33 questions, freeze them, and report on the other 31. Five metrics, most scored automatically because the dataset records expected citations, required values and known-wrong values in advance; claim precision needs manual labels. We compare against four baselines: plain RAG with no verification, the LLM checking its own claims, MiniCheck as the verifier, and our system without the numeric guard.

**Likely questions:**

- **How will you get ground truth for claim precision?** Claims are generated fresh on each run, so they can't be labelled in advance. We'll label kept claims on the held-out split manually.
- **Isn't 64 questions small?** Yes, and we'll say so in the results. It's hand-written to target specific failure types, and the held-out split keeps tuning from inflating the numbers.
- **Why count over-refusal as a failure?** A system that refuses everything has zero hallucinations and is useless. 49 answerable questions make false abstention measurable.

## 11. Conclusion & References

**Speaker notes:**

To summarise: we propose answering policy questions claim by claim, with every claim checked at answer time by a model independent of the generator, rejected claims shown with reasons, and abstention when evidence runs out. The full pipeline is built and runs locally; the evaluation is our next milestone, so we're not claiming any accuracy yet. Before evaluating, we'll fix four issues our own code audit found. The references are the six papers from our literature survey, plus the models and data we use.

**Likely questions:**

- **What did the audit find?** Citation-error type isn't carried per claim through the pipeline; the 0.25 retrieval threshold never triggers on our question set; the model only sees the first 1,200 characters of each passage; and the number check ignores 0–2 and the word 'percent'.
- **When will you have results?** After the evaluation harness is built. All numbers will be reported on the held-out split.
- **What's the expected outcome?** Fewer unsupported claims reaching the user than plain RAG, at a measured cost in abstentions. That is a hypothesis we will test, not a result.
